Old regime or new regime?

India lets most taxpayers choose between two tax regimes each year. Neither is universally "better" — the right one depends on your income and the deductions you actually use. Understanding the trade-off is the first step to being tax-smart.

The Old Regime

Generally has higher slab rates but allows a wide range of deductions and exemptions (such as 80C, 80D, HRA, home-loan interest and more). It tends to suit those who actively use several deductions.

The New Regime

Generally offers lower slab rates but removes most deductions and exemptions. It tends to suit those who claim few deductions and prefer simplicity.

How to decide

The practical method is simple: estimate your tax under both regimes for the year, including the deductions you would genuinely claim, and compare. The regime that leaves more in your pocket, for your actual situation, is the one to choose. Because slab rates, rebates and rules are revised from time to time (often in the annual Budget), this is a calculation worth redoing each year with current figures.

Important. This guide deliberately does not state specific slab rates, rebate thresholds or limits, because they change and any figure here could become outdated. Always check the current rates and limits for the relevant assessment year, and run the comparison with a qualified tax professional.
The key principle. Choose investments that suit your goals and risk profile first, then structure them tax-efficiently. A product bought only to save tax, but which does not suit you, is rarely a good outcome — don't let the tax tail wag the investment dog.

The deduction toolbox

These are commonly used deductions available under the old regime (most are not available under the new regime). They are listed for awareness — this is not a recommendation to use any of them, and eligibility, conditions and limits must be verified against current law.

Stay on track all year

Annual Financial Planning Calendar

A simple month-by-month rhythm for the Indian financial year (April–March). Dates for filings and statements vary year to year — treat these as reminders to check, not exact deadlines.

APR
New year reset. Choose your tax regime for the year; restart/step up SIPs early so compounding gets the full year; set this year's goals.
MAY–JUN
Document gather. Collect Form 16, interest certificates and capital-gains statements; reconcile your AIS / Form 26AS.
JUL
Income-tax return. File your ITR within the applicable due date (verify the current date); don't wait for the last week.
AUG–SEP
Mid-year review. Check progress against goals; review your emergency fund and insurance cover for any gaps.
OCT
Portfolio check. Review your overall asset mix; consider whether it has drifted from your intended allocation.
NOV–DEC
Tax planning, not panic. Estimate your annual tax early; plan any eligible deductions calmly rather than rushing in March.
JAN
Proof submission. Submit investment / deduction proofs to your employer if salaried; verify what's been recorded.
FEB
Budget watch. Note any changes announced in the Union Budget that may affect your plan for the next year.
MAR
Year-end close. Complete any remaining eligible actions before 31 March; record the year's transactions for filing.
The habit that matters. Spreading these tasks across the year — instead of cramming them into March — leads to calmer, better decisions and avoids last-minute mistakes.
